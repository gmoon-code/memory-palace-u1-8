# V2 Narrative Scene Standard

Every future Memory Palace scene must function as a piece of narrative first and a mnemonic container second. The learner should be able to picture the location, identify what is present, understand what happens, and connect the event to the scientific term without needing to understand the app's mnemonic architecture.

## Required scene anatomy

### 1. Exact physical location

Each scene must state where the learner is and how that location relates to the surrounding route. Avoid vague settings such as “in the room” or “at the station.”

A scene should specify details such as:

- floor or level
- entry direction
- central landmark
- exit direction
- relationship to the previous and next locus

### 2. Simple spatial layout

Every scene uses three principal spatial zones. In the reference implementation these are displayed as left, center, and right. The purpose is orientation, not decoration.

The learner should be able to close their eyes and answer:

- What is on my left?
- What is directly in front of me?
- What is on my right?
- Where do I go next?

### 3. Stable guide

A guide may recur across the palace. The guide should have a stable identity, appearance, and story function. The guide moves the plot or directs attention. The guide must not become a lecturer who stops the story to recite definitions.

In The Flooded Sky Hotel, Mara Vale is the chief systems engineer. She opens damaged systems, activates molecular views, and directs attention to what the water is doing.

### 4. Clear scientific cast

When a scene contains scientific parts or actors, each receives three pieces of information:

- **name** — the scientific term
- **visual identity** — what the learner should picture
- **job** — what the part does in the scientific system

The job must describe a real scientific relationship. Decorative personalities do not substitute for function.

### 5. Causal narrative action

The story event should demonstrate the science. If the learner remembers what happened, the event should help reconstruct the scientific relationship.

Examples:

- oxygen pulls shared electrons more strongly → uneven partial charges → water polarity
- water attracts water → cohesion
- water attracts a different polar surface → adhesion
- cohesion + adhesion in a narrow space → capillary action

### 6. Natural term introduction

The term should appear at the moment the learner has enough visual and causal information to understand it.

Preferred pattern:

> The oxygen side is δ− and the hydrogen sides are δ+. The molecule now has an uneven distribution of charge. That unevenness is **water polarity**.

Avoid:

> The term you need to memorize is water polarity.

### 7. One concise meaning after the experience

After the narrative, the scene may show a compact memory anchor. It should reinforce the term, meaning, and image already experienced in the story. It should not introduce an additional lecture.

### 8. Causal transition to the next locus

The scene should end with an event that physically or narratively moves the learner to the next location. Avoid generic transitions such as “now go to the next room.”

### 9. No source-facing meta language

Student-visible prose must not include phrases such as:

- “the PPT states”
- “the CED says”
- “the course requires”
- “the locked definition is”
- “the source material shows”

Source provenance remains in the scientific/content layer and teacher-facing QA. The student experiences the biology directly.

### 10. Recall must not destroy the story flow

First exposure should contain few interruptions. Quick Recall is optional during first exposure in guided journeys. When opened, the full story, route label, and memory anchors are hidden so the retrieval is genuine.

The learner sees only:

- one natural question
- “I remembered it”
- “Give me one hint”
- an option to return or continue

## Narrative-quality questions

A scene does not pass merely because every scientific fact is present. Before release, ask:

1. Can a learner sketch the physical scene from the prose?
2. Does every important object have a stable position?
3. Is it clear what each scientific part is?
4. Is it clear what each scientific part does?
5. Does the action demonstrate the meaning of the term?
6. Does the scene create a reason to move to the next locus?
7. Would the story still make sense if the labels were temporarily hidden?
8. Is memorable detail serving the science or competing with it?
9. Are there unnecessary characters, jokes, or decorative events?
10. Does the learner know what happened and why?
