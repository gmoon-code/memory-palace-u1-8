## Scene-by-scene design

### 1. Tonicity Orientation Hall

**Narrative title**  A label that means nothing without the cell

**Location**  Tonicity Orientation Hall, the circular conservatory entrance where a pale hypotonic chamber is fixed on your left, the unchanged transparent reference cell sits at center, and isotonic and hypertonic comparison doors remain fixed on your right.

**Story length**  485 words

**Spatial map**

- **Left · Hypotonic door** — A blue glass door is labeled with an outside solution containing fewer effective nonpenetrating solute particles than the transparent reference cell in the center.
- **Center · Reference cell tank** — One transparent reference cell sits in a sealed tank with its internal nonpenetrating-solute concentration held constant throughout the comparison.
- **Right · Comparison doors** — Two additional doors lead to an isotonic room and a hypertonic room, each changing the outside solution while leaving the reference cell itself unchanged.

**Scientific cast**

- **reference cell** — a transparent animal cell with a fixed internal concentration of nonpenetrating solute. Job: serve as the comparison point that makes every outside solution label relative rather than absolute.
- **solution doors** — three color-coded glass doors containing different external nonpenetrating-solute concentrations. Job: change only the environment around the same reference cell.
- **tonicity gauge** — a three-way meter labeled WATER GAIN, NO NET CHANGE, and WATER LOSS. Job: translate the outside solution comparison into the cell response tonicity predicts.

**Terms / relationships taught**

- **Tonicity relationships** — An external solution may be hypotonic, hypertonic, or isotonic relative to a cell interior.
- **Tonicity** — Tonicity describes how a solution containing nonpenetrating solutes causes a cell to gain or lose water.

**Carry forward**  Hypotonic, isotonic, and hypertonic are relative to the cell and predict water gain/loss.

**Optional Quick Recall**  Tonicity predicts what aspect of a cell’s behavior?

### 2. Isotonic Balance Pool

**Narrative title**  A still cell with moving water

**Location**  Isotonic Balance Pool, a quiet glass chamber where water enters from the left, the same transparent reference cell remains centered at stable average volume, and a matching stream of water exits to the right.

**Story length**  333 words

**Spatial map**

- **Left · Water entering** — Silver water molecules continually cross the membrane from the external solution into the reference cell; the inward counter records every crossing.
- **Center · Reference cell** — The transparent animal cell remains at nearly constant average volume even though individual water molecules keep crossing its plasma membrane.
- **Right · Water leaving** — A matching stream of water molecules continually leaves the cell; the outward counter balances the inward counter over time.

**Scientific cast**

- **reference cell** — the same transparent animal cell from the orientation hall, now floating at stable average volume. Job: show that stable volume can coexist with continual molecular movement.
- **water molecules** — silver droplets crossing the membrane in both directions. Job: make dynamic water exchange visible at isotonic balance.
- **crossing counters** — paired inward and outward counters displaying equal long-term rates. Job: separate zero net water movement from zero molecular movement.

**Terms / relationships taught**

- **Isotonic solution** — In an isotonic environment, there is no net water movement across the membrane, although water continues moving in both directions.

**Carry forward**  Isotonic = water still moves both ways, but there is no net water movement.

### 3. Hypertonic Withdrawal Chamber

**Narrative title**  When the outside pulls the balance outward

**Location**  Hypertonic Withdrawal Chamber, where the concentrated external solution occupies the left side, the same animal cell shrinks at center inside its original outline, and the net water-flow display points outward on the right.

**Story length**  353 words

**Spatial map**

- **Left · Hypertonic solution** — The external chamber contains a greater effective concentration of nonpenetrating solute than the animal cell interior, making the comparison visibly unequal.
- **Center · Shrinking animal cell** — The same flexible reference cell loses water and decreases in volume because it has no rigid cell wall resisting the change.
- **Right · Net water path** — A bright stream of water arrows points outward from the cell, showing the net result while smaller arrows still indicate molecular movement in both directions.

**Scientific cast**

- **reference cell** — the same flexible animal cell, outlined with a volume grid. Job: shrink as net water leaves in the hypertonic environment.
- **external nonpenetrating solute** — dense red solute particles that remain outside the cell during the demonstration. Job: create the hypertonic comparison without crossing the membrane.
- **water-flow display** — large net-outward arrow layered over smaller two-way molecular arrows. Job: show net water loss without implying that every water molecule moves only outward.

**Terms / relationships taught**

- **Hypertonic solution** — A cell in a hypertonic environment loses water; animal cells shrink and plant cells may plasmolyze. Water loss does not necessarily mean immediate cell death.

**Carry forward**  Hypertonic outside → net water loss from the cell.

### 4. Hypotonic Plant Greenhouse

**Narrative title**  The same incoming water, two different boundaries

**Location**  Hypotonic Plant Greenhouse, a three-bay room with a swelling animal cell on the left, a turgid plant cell pressing against its wall at center, and a turgid-versus-plasmolyzed plant comparison fixed on the right.

**Story length**  419 words

**Spatial map**

- **Left · Swelling animal cell** — A membrane-bound animal cell receives net water, expands visibly, and approaches lysis because no cell wall resists continuing expansion.
- **Center · Turgid plant cell** — A plant cell takes in water until its contents press firmly against the cellulose wall; a pressure gauge rises as the wall resists further expansion.
- **Right · Plant-state comparison** — A split panel compares the same plant cell when turgid with its hypertonic state, where water loss causes the plasma membrane to pull away from the wall.

**Scientific cast**

- **animal cell** — a flexible membrane-bound cell without a rigid wall. Job: swell and potentially lyse as net water enters in the hypotonic environment.
- **plant cell** — a green plant cell with a visible plasma membrane inside a rigid cellulose wall. Job: become turgid when incoming water presses the cell contents against the wall.
- **turgor gauge** — a pressure meter between the plant-cell contents and the wall. Job: make turgor pressure rise as the wall resists expansion.
- **plasmolysis panel** — the same plant cell shown after water loss with its membrane pulled away from the wall. Job: contrast plasmolysis with the turgid state and keep the directions straight.

**Terms / relationships taught**

- **Hypotonic solution** — A cell in a hypotonic environment gains water; animal cells can swell and lyse, while a healthy plant cell becomes turgid as the cell wall resists expansion.
- **Plasmolysis** — Plasmolysis occurs when a plant cell loses water and the plasma membrane pulls away from the cell wall.
- **Turgor pressure** — Turgor pressure is the pressure generated as water enters a plant cell and the cell contents press against the cell wall; it helps support nonwoody plant tissues.

**Carry forward**  Hypotonic water entry swells animal cells but makes healthy plant cells turgid because the wall resists expansion.

### 5. Osmosis and Water-Potential River

**Narrative title**  Replace the solute slogan with the water-potential rule

**Location**  Osmosis and Water-Potential River, a transparent two-compartment channel where the higher-water-potential reservoir is on the left, the selectively permeable divider is centered beneath the bridge, and the lower-water-potential reservoir is on the right.

**Story length**  393 words

**Spatial map**

- **Left · Higher water potential** — A transparent compartment is marked with the higher total water potential; silver water molecules are free to move from this side toward the divider.
- **Center · Selective divider** — A selectively permeable membrane separates the compartments, allowing free water to cross while the demonstration keeps selected solutes restricted.
- **Right · Lower water potential** — The second compartment is marked with lower water potential; the net water-flow counter points toward this side while individual water molecules still move both ways.

**Scientific cast**

- **free water** — silver water molecules that can cross the selectively permeable divider. Job: move net from higher total water potential toward lower total water potential.
- **selectively permeable membrane** — a transparent divider that permits water movement while restricting selected solute particles. Job: create the membrane condition required for the osmosis demonstration.
- **water-potential markers** — large Ψ displays showing HIGH on the left and LOW on the right. Job: replace the oversimplified more-solute slogan with the general directional rule.
- **reference and plant cells** — miniature versions of the cells from the previous chambers placed beside the river. Job: connect the river rule back to the swelling, shrinking, and turgor outcomes already observed.

**Terms / relationships taught**

- **Osmosis and water potential** — Water moves by osmosis from regions of higher water potential to regions of lower water potential; this often corresponds to movement from hypotonic toward hypertonic regions.
- **Osmolarity direction** — When other components are equal, water tends to move from lower osmolarity/solute concentration toward higher osmolarity/solute concentration.
- **Osmosis** — Osmosis is net diffusion of free water across a selectively permeable membrane from higher water potential toward lower water potential.
- **Membrane movement and homeostasis** — Growth and homeostasis depend on continual movement of molecules across membranes.

**Carry forward**  Osmosis follows water potential; higher solute concentration predicts direction only when other components are equal.

### 6. Water-Potential Control Room

**Narrative title**  Two controls determine where the water goes

**Location**  Water-Potential Control Room, the loft above the river where the Ψp pressure piston is fixed on the left, the equations Ψ = Ψp + Ψs and Ψs = −iCRT fill the center console, and solute/osmoregulation controls occupy the right wall.

**Story length**  530 words

**Spatial map**

- **Left · Pressure control** — A piston and pressure gauge let Nia raise or lower the physical pressure contribution to water potential; an open beaker reference can be set to relative Ψp = 0.
- **Center · Water-potential console** — A large transparent console combines pressure potential and solute potential, while a lower display shows the solute-potential equation Ψs = −iCRT.
- **Right · Solute and balance controls** — A solute injector makes Ψs more negative and an osmoregulation controller adjusts water and solute conditions to stabilize the modeled internal environment.

**Scientific cast**

- **pressure-potential piston** — a movable piston connected to a gauge labeled Ψp. Job: change the physical pressure component of total water potential.
- **solute injector** — a calibrated injector adding dissolved solute beside a display labeled Ψs. Job: show that adding solute makes solute potential more negative.
- **water-potential console** — a central illuminated equation board reading Ψ = Ψp + Ψs and Ψs = −iCRT. Job: combine the two components and make the quantitative relationships explicit.
- **osmoregulation controller** — a feedback panel adjusting water and solute balance around a transparent model cell. Job: show active regulation of internal water balance and solute composition.

**Terms / relationships taught**

- **Water potential equation** — Water potential is the sum of pressure potential and solute potential: Ψ = Ψp + Ψs.
- **Solute potential equation** — Solute potential can be calculated as Ψs = −iCRT, with i as ionization/van’t Hoff factor, C molar concentration, R pressure constant, and T Kelvin temperature.
- **Pressure potential** — Pressure potential is the physical pressure component of water potential; in an open beaker at atmospheric pressure it is conventionally treated as zero relative pressure.
- **Solute potential** — Adding dissolved solute lowers solute potential; solute potential is zero for pure water under the standard convention and is negative for solutions.
- **Osmoregulation** — Osmoregulation maintains water balance and permits control of internal solute composition and water potential.

**Carry forward**  Ψ = Ψp + Ψs; adding solute lowers Ψs; pressure can also change total water potential.
