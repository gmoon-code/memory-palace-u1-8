## The Archive That Counted DNA Twice

The archive has confused packaging, chromosome identity, homologs, ploidy, and DNA replication. Follow one chromosome set from chromatin through G1, S, G2, and G0 until the count is trustworthy enough for mitosis.

**Guide**  Dr. Mira Chen, cellular-systems investigator

**Premise**  The cell-division archive has filed histones, nucleosomes, chromatin, chromosomes, sister chromatids, and homologs under overlapping labels. Its worst error appears after S phase, when doubled DNA is being reported as doubled chromosome number. Mira places one color-coded eukaryotic chromosome set in a transparent archive case and refuses to swap it for a new model. The same identities must survive every change in packaging and DNA amount.

**Mission**  Repair the archive in order. Build DNA packing from histones and nucleosomes into chromatin and genome-scale organization; map replicated chromosome anatomy; separate homologs, somatic/gamete roles, and diploid/haploid set number; establish the cell-cycle clock; then carry the same chromosome set through G1, S, G2, and the G0 branch while tracking exactly what changes and what does not.

**Route**  Genome Packing Archive → Chromosome Anatomy Workbench → Chromosome Sets Gallery → Cell-Cycle Clock → G1 Growth Gate → S-Phase Replication Room → G2 Preparation Bay → G0 Side Chamber

## 1. Genome Packing Archive

### The Archive With One Label for Everything

*DNA can be packaged at several structural levels without changing what material it is.*

**Physical layout**  On your left is DNA and histone components. Directly ahead is Nucleosome and chromatin packing hierarchy. On your right is Genome-scale organization. These anchors remain fixed while the chromosome/cell-cycle mechanism changes.

A narrow bridge leaves the Feedback Regulation Center and ends at a brass door stamped **CELL-DIVISION ARCHIVE**. When it opens, a strip of loose DNA is hanging halfway out of a filing drawer like a ribbon that has been stuffed into the wrong cabinet. On your **left**, a long molecular model of DNA lies beside separate histone protein cores. Directly **ahead**, a magnification rail has three positions: one wrapped unit, a longer packed fiber, and a chromosome-scale model. On your **right**, an enormous wall map is labeled GENETIC MATERIAL, but every scale beneath it—from one wrapped unit to the whole collection—has been stamped with the same red label: CHROMOSOME COPY. Mira sets a transparent archive case on the center rail. Inside is the chromosome set you will carry through the rest of the journey, marked with artificial blue-and-gold tracing bands so you can follow identities without pretending chromosomes are naturally those colors.

Mira begins at the left table. She lays one DNA segment across a cluster of **histones**, proteins associated with eukaryotic DNA. The DNA bends around the histone core instead of remaining as an endless loose strand. Once the wrap is visible, she names the unit a **nucleosome**: DNA wrapped around a core of histone proteins. She slides the model onto the center rail and joins many such units into a longer DNA-protein structure. That material is **chromatin**. The label does not mean 'loose DNA only.' Chromatin is the DNA-protein complex that constitutes eukaryotic chromosomes, and its degree of compaction changes through the cell cycle. Mira keeps the histone cores visible inside the packed model so **chromatin composition** stays concrete: eukaryotic chromatin consists primarily of DNA associated with proteins, especially histones.

The magnification rail now pulls backward. One nucleosome becomes many. The chromatin fiber occupies more of the display. Then the view widens to chromosome-scale organization. Mira stops the archive before it can repeat its old mistake. 'Condensation changes the packaging,' she says, tapping the same blue tracing band at every magnification. 'It does not create a chromosome out of material that was somehow not a chromosome before.' A chromosome is represented by its DNA molecule with associated proteins throughout the cell cycle; during mitosis the chromatin becomes much more condensed and therefore easier to see as distinct chromosomes. The archive had confused visibility with existence.

Finally, Mira turns to the right wall and lights the outermost boundary around the complete genetic collection. That complete set of genetic material is the **genome**. The wall now reads from small scale to large without substituting one term for another: histone proteins help package DNA; DNA wrapped around a histone core forms a nucleosome; nucleosomes contribute to chromatin; chromatin is the DNA-protein material of chromosomes; and the genome refers to the complete genetic material. The labels remain separate while the material stays causally connected. Nothing new has been invented just because the view zoomed out.

A drawer on the right unlocks and ejects one chromosome-scale model into Mira's transparent case. It is clearly replicated, with two matching elongated halves joined near the middle, but the archive printer immediately spits out TWO CHROMOSOMES. Mira tears off the incorrect ticket. 'Packing is fixed,' she says. 'Counting is next.' She carries the same model through the only open door, where a bright work lamp illuminates the **Chromosome Anatomy Workbench**.

**Exit**  The archive now distinguishes histone, nucleosome, chromatin, chromosome-scale packing, and genome without replacing one with another. The unresolved replicated model has been physically carried into the next room because its parts must be identified before any chromosome number can be trusted.

## 2. Chromosome Anatomy Workbench

### The X That Was Still One Chromosome

*A replicated chromosome contains two sister chromatids, yet before separation it is still one chromosome when counted by its centromere.*

**Physical layout**  On your left is First sister chromatid. Directly ahead is Centromere and kinetochore region. On your right is Second sister chromatid. These anchors remain fixed while the chromosome/cell-cycle mechanism changes.

The door closes behind you and a white work lamp snaps on over the **Chromosome Anatomy Workbench**. Mira places the replicated model flat across the bench so its geometry cannot drift. One elongated half lies on your **left**. The narrow joining region sits directly **ahead** at the center of the bench. The second elongated half lies on your **right**. The archive's red TWO CHROMOSOMES ticket is still caught under the case lid. Above the table, a mechanical counter has three columns labeled DNA COPIES, CHROMATIDS, and CHROMOSOMES. The first two columns read 2. The third is flashing an error because the archive has been counting every copied DNA-protein strand as an independent chromosome too early.

Mira first names the whole structure. A **chromosome** is a DNA molecule with associated proteins that carries genetic information. After DNA replication, the model is a **replicated chromosome**. It now contains two copies made by replication, but those copies remain physically joined. Mira points to the left half and then the right. Each is a **sister chromatid**. Sister chromatids are replicated DNA-protein copies of the same chromosome, held together until they separate during anaphase. She clips the two tracing bands to the model so you can follow both halves without confusing them with homologous chromosomes, which have not entered this room yet.

At the center, Mira removes the generic 'middle' sticker and exposes a specialized chromosome region. This is the **centromere**. It helps hold the sister chromatids together and provides the region where another structure will assemble. A small protein complex then locks onto the centromere area of the left chromatid, and a matching complex appears on the right. Each one is a **kinetochore**. The kinetochore is not the centromere itself. It is a protein complex assembled at the centromere of each chromatid, and later spindle microtubules will attach there. Mira makes you point twice: region first, protein complex second. The centromere belongs to the chromosome structure; the kinetochore is built on that region.

Now she resets the counter. The model contains two sister chromatids, yet before those sisters separate it is still treated as one replicated chromosome when chromosome number is counted by centromeres. Mira rotates the model ninety degrees and then back again. Left sister chromatid. Center centromere region with kinetochore. Right sister chromatid. Nothing about the rotation changes those relationships. She deliberately leaves a second, unrelated chromosome outside the room so you do not confuse this pair of sister chromatids with a homologous pair.

The workbench finally prints ONE REPLICATED CHROMOSOME, TWO SISTER CHROMATIDS. A side wall slides open, revealing two tall display stands carrying corresponding chromosomes that resemble each other but do not share the same tracing band. Mira lifts the anatomy model into the case. 'You know the parts of one replicated chromosome,' she says. 'Now we need to know which chromosomes belong to the same set and which are copies of each other.' The new doorway opens into the **Chromosome Sets Gallery**.

**Exit**  The replicated chromosome leaves the workbench with one sister chromatid on each side, a centromere region in the middle, and separate kinetochore complexes assembled there. The gallery ahead is necessary because sister-copy identity alone cannot explain homologous chromosomes, chromosome sets, or ploidy.

## 3. Chromosome Sets Gallery

### The Gallery of Pairs That Were Not Copies

*Homologous chromosomes correspond in gene organization, while sister chromatids are replicated copies of one chromosome.*

**Physical layout**  On your left is Prokaryotic and haploid-side examples. Directly ahead is Homologous chromosome-set comparison. On your right is Diploid somatic and gamete relationships. These anchors remain fixed while the chromosome/cell-cycle mechanism changes.

The **Chromosome Sets Gallery** is wider than the workbench and divided into three permanent exhibits. On your **left** is a small nucleoid display beside a single-set cell model. Directly **ahead**, two long chromosomes stand side by side with corresponding gene-locus markers aligned down their lengths. On your **right**, two transparent cell cases are stacked vertically: a body-cell example above and a reproductive-cell example below. Mira places the replicated chromosome from the previous room beside the center pair and immediately removes one of its sister chromatids from the comparison line. 'Copies and homologs are different relationships,' she says. 'If we blur them now, every ploidy calculation later becomes unreliable.'

She starts at the left exhibit. A model prokaryotic cell shows a main circular chromosome in a nucleoid region. Mira labels this a **common prokaryotic chromosome pattern**, then leaves a bright exception tag beside it: many prokaryotes have a main circular chromosome, but chromosome number and topology vary among species. The next panel contrasts the **eukaryotic chromosome pattern**: eukaryotic nuclear genomes are organized into one or more linear chromosomes. The shapes are useful comparisons, but neither display is allowed to become an absolute rule that erases biological variation.

At the center, Mira brings the two tall chromosomes together. Their gene-locus markers line up in corresponding positions, but one locus carries a different allele marker from its partner. These are **homologous chromosomes**. Homologs carry corresponding gene loci and similar structural organization, yet they can contain different alleles. Mira then places the replicated chromosome from the previous room beside them. Its two sister chromatids are copies made by DNA replication. The homologs are corresponding chromosome partners, commonly one set inherited from each biological parent in a sexually reproducing diploid organism. The gallery lights the contrast: homologous does not mean identical copy, and sister chromatid does not mean homolog.

The right-side upper case now opens. It represents a **somatic cell**, a non-gamete body cell. In the diploid example, two homologous chromosome sets are present. Mira writes **diploid, 2n**, beside the case: diploid cells contain two chromosome sets. In sexually reproducing diploid organisms, one set is inherited from each biological parent. She then opens the lower case. It contains one chromosome set and is labeled **haploid, n**. A **gamete** is a haploid reproductive cell produced through meiosis or meiotic processes. Mira draws a one-way arrow from a meiotic process into the gamete case and refuses to reverse it. The mature gamete is the product here; it is not being defined as a cell that itself divides by meiosis.

To test the filing system, Mira hands you four labels: SOMATIC, DIPLOID, GAMETE, HAPLOID. They are related but not synonyms. A somatic cell describes cell role; diploid describes chromosome-set number. Gamete describes a reproductive cell type; haploid describes one chromosome set. In the model diploid organism, most somatic cells are diploid and gametes are haploid, but the words answer different questions. The archive printer finally stops merging them into one label.

A circular clock begins ticking behind the gallery wall. The same chromosome set is loaded into a transparent cell case and placed at the top of the clock. The dial contains G1, S, G2, and a division arc, but its old records have stamped 'INTERPHASE = RESTING' across most of the face. Mira turns toward the opening. 'We know what the chromosomes are and how many sets we have,' she says. 'Now we follow what the cell actually does with them over time.' You enter the **Cell-Cycle Clock**.

**Exit**  The gallery leaves four distinctions intact: common prokaryotic and eukaryotic chromosome organization, homologs versus sister chromatids, somatic/gamete roles, and diploid/haploid set number. The same chromosome set now enters the cell-cycle clock so those identities can be followed through time rather than relearned as new objects.

## 4. Cell-Cycle Clock

### The Clock That Called Interphase a Pause

*Interphase is active G1, S, and G2, while M phase coordinates chromosome segregation with cell division.*

**Physical layout**  On your left is G1–S–G2 interphase arc. Directly ahead is Cell-cycle control clock. On your right is M-phase division arc. These anchors remain fixed while the chromosome/cell-cycle mechanism changes.

The **Cell-Cycle Clock** is a circular rotunda with a floor dial large enough to walk around. The broad **left** arc is divided into G1, S, and G2. A brass hub turns directly **ahead** at the center and advances one transparent cell case around the dial. On the **right** is a smaller division arc marked M. The archive's old sign hangs over the left arc: INTERPHASE—CELL RESTS HERE. Mira unhooks it and lays it face down. Inside the moving case, the same chromosome set from the gallery remains visible, ready to change state only when the biology actually changes.

Mira names the whole regulated sequence the **cell cycle**. The way this sequence is controlled is **cell cycle regulation**: the cell cycle is a highly regulated series of events controlling growth and reproduction of eukaryotic cells. The center clock is not merely a timer. It is a route through ordered biological events. The cell case moves first through G1, then S, then G2. Together these stages make up **interphase**. These ordered **cell-cycle stages** place interphase as G1, S, and G2 before the division events of mitosis and cytokinesis. Interphase is commonly the longest portion of the cell cycle, but its duration varies widely among cells and conditions. Mira refuses to paint '90%' across the dial. A rough classroom proportion can be useful in a particular example, but it is not a universal law of every cell cycle.

As the case passes along the left arc, nothing looks like a resting cell. In G1 the cell grows and functions. In S phase DNA is replicated. In G2 the cell continues preparation for division. Mira therefore replaces RESTING with ACTIVE PREPARATION AND REPLICATION. Then the case reaches the right-hand arc. **M phase** includes mitosis and usually cytokinesis, coordinating nuclear chromosome segregation with cell division. Mira keeps mitosis and cytokinesis visible as related processes without pretending they are the same event. The details of chromosome movement will be handled in the next journey; this clock only establishes where that division machinery belongs in the larger sequence.

The clock advances one full cycle and then stops at the G1 entrance. Mira points to the chromosome case. After a completed cell division, the new cell begins with chromosomes that are not yet replicated into sister-chromatid pairs for the next cycle. The next three rooms will follow what happens to that same set through G1, S, and G2. The clock's broad interphase arc is therefore not an empty waiting room. It is where growth, DNA replication, and preparation occur in an ordered sequence.

A gate on the left arc rises only at G1. Beyond it you can see a spacious work floor, while the S-phase copying machinery remains sealed behind another wall. Mira steps through the opening before the central clock can skip ahead. 'One phase at a time,' she says. 'If DNA copies in the wrong room, our count will fail again.' The tracked cell follows her into the **G1 Growth Gate**.

**Exit**  The clock now defines the regulated cell cycle and separates interphase from M phase without calling interphase inactive. The tracked cell leaves at G1 because the next task is to establish what grows and functions before DNA replication begins.

## 5. G1 Growth Gate

### The Growth Floor With the Copier Locked

*G1 is active growth and cellular function before S-phase DNA replication.*

**Physical layout**  On your left is Post-division cell. Directly ahead is G1 growth and cellular activity zone. On your right is Boundary toward DNA synthesis. These anchors remain fixed while the chromosome/cell-cycle mechanism changes.

The **G1 Growth Gate** opens onto a bright rectangular room. The newly divided cell enters on your **left** with the tracked chromosome set still unreplicated. Directly **ahead**, the center floor is crowded with ribosomes, membranes, organelles, cytosolic components, and ordinary cellular workstations. On your **right**, a heavy door marked S stands closed, and through its glass you can see DNA-copying machinery that is deliberately powered off. Mira clips the archive case beside the left wall so every time the cell grows you can check whether its chromosomes have been copied. They have not.

The cell begins increasing in size and producing cellular material. Mira names this interval **G1 phase**. During G1, the cell is metabolically active. It grows, duplicates organelles and cytosolic components, and carries out normal cellular functions. The room becomes visibly busier without adding a second sister chromatid to any chromosome. That visual separation is the point. Growth of the cell and duplication of cellular components do not mean the nuclear DNA has already undergone S-phase replication.

Mira walks you to the locked right-hand door and points through the glass. 'The copier belongs next door,' she says. The chromosome case still shows one chromatid per tracked chromosome. No sister pairs have appeared. The scene therefore leaves G1 with a simple picture: a functioning, growing cell on the center floor and an unreplicated chromosome set on the left. Mira briefly moves the cell case from the entrance rail to the far edge of the growth floor and then back again; the chromosome models remain unreplicated in both positions. The upcoming S phase is a different biochemical event with a different consequence for DNA content. That contrast is deliberately repeated so ordinary biomass increase cannot be mistaken for chromosome replication.

A small decision panel sits beside the exit, but Mira covers most of it with her tablet. Later, the Cell-Cycle Security Headquarters will deal with the G1 checkpoint and the signals that influence progression. Here, the phase itself remains distinct from a checkpoint inside its control system. G1 is a period of cellular growth and activity; it is not defined as 'the checkpoint.' This prevents a regulatory event from replacing the entire phase in memory.

The cell reaches the right-hand boundary with enough cellular material to continue. The S door unlocks, and a narrow conveyor accepts the same chromosome case. Before it moves, Mira zeroes three counters above the doorway: DNA CONTENT, CHROMATID NUMBER, and CHROMOSOME NUMBER. 'Watch all three,' she says. 'Only one of these is where people usually make the wrong double.' The conveyor carries the unreplicated set into the **S-Phase Replication Room**.

**Exit**  G1 ends with a metabolically active, grown cell and an unreplicated chromosome set. The DNA-copying machinery is still confined to the next room, making the G1-to-S boundary physically clear before any counting changes occur.

## 6. S-Phase Replication Room

### The Copier That Doubled DNA but Not the Chromosome Count

*S phase doubles DNA content and chromatid number while centromere-counted chromosome number remains unchanged before sister chromatids separate.*

**Physical layout**  On your left is Unreplicated chromosome set. Directly ahead is S-phase DNA replication machinery. On your right is Replicated set with unchanged chromosome count. These anchors remain fixed while the chromosome/cell-cycle mechanism changes.

The conveyor delivers the archive case onto the **left** platform of the **S-Phase Replication Room**. Each tracked chromosome is still unreplicated, and the three counters above it show baseline values. Directly **ahead**, a transparent copying tunnel surrounds the chromosome set. On the **right**, an empty receiving platform holds a large sign with three blank spaces: DNA CONTENT, CHROMATIDS, CHROMOSOMES. Mira locks the room so the case cannot leave until every number is reconciled. This is the error that first corrupted the archive.

The copying tunnel activates. During **S phase**, DNA replicates. Each chromosome's DNA molecule is copied so the chromosome emerges in a replicated state containing two sister chromatids joined at the centromere. The artificial tracing bands make the relationship visible: the new sister copy carries the same chromosome identity as its partner because it arose by replication of that chromosome. The process does not create a new homologous chromosome pair. It creates sister chromatids within each replicated chromosome.

Now the counters update. DNA CONTENT doubles because the amount of DNA has doubled. CHROMATID NUMBER also doubles because each unreplicated chromosome now has two sister chromatids. The CHROMOSOME NUMBER counter tries to double as well, but Mira stops it. Before sister chromatids separate, chromosome number counted by centromeres remains the same. This is the **chromosome counting across S phase** rule the archive had violated: DNA replication doubles DNA content and chromatid number, not the centromere-counted chromosome number at this stage.

Mira demonstrates with one model. Before replication: one chromosome, one chromatid, one DNA molecule in that chromosome. After replication: one replicated chromosome, two sister chromatids, two DNA molecules associated with that chromosome. She points to the shared chromosome identity and the centromere region that keeps the sisters connected. The exact numerical totals depend on the starting cell, but the relationship does not. DNA amount can change without chromosome number changing in the same way.

The right platform fills with replicated chromosomes prepared for later segregation. Mira intentionally does not separate the sister chromatids here. That event belongs to anaphase in the next journey. Only after sister chromatids separate do the former chromatids become individual daughter chromosomes, and that later change affects chromosome counting. For now, the case leaves S phase with twice the DNA and twice the chromatids, while chromosome number by centromeres is unchanged.

The three counters finally agree and the receiving door opens. Beyond it is a broad preparation bay with no DNA-copying tunnel. Mira carries the replicated case forward. 'Replication happened once,' she says. 'Now the cell has to prepare to move what it copied.' You enter the **G2 Preparation Bay** with sister chromatids intact.

**Exit**  The S-phase room resolves the bookkeeping error: DNA content and chromatid number double, while centromere-counted chromosome number stays constant before sister-chromatid separation. The replicated set now moves into G2 without undergoing another round of DNA replication.

## 7. G2 Preparation Bay

### The Preparation Bay That Refused a Second Copy

*G2 prepares a replicated cell for mitosis after DNA synthesis is already complete.*

**Physical layout**  On your left is Completed DNA replication. Directly ahead is G2 growth and mitotic preparation. On your right is Prepared entry toward mitosis. These anchors remain fixed while the chromosome/cell-cycle mechanism changes.

The replicated chromosome case enters the **G2 Preparation Bay** on your **left**. Two sister chromatids are still visible for every tracked replicated chromosome. Directly **ahead**, the central bay is filled with protein-synthesis stations, structural components, and division-preparation equipment. On your **right**, a tall gate marked M leads toward the Mitosis Transit Hall, but it remains closed. Mira looks back at the S-phase door behind you. Its copying tunnel has shut down. No second DNA-replication machine exists in this room.

Mira names the phase **G2**. During G2, the cell continues protein synthesis and prepares for mitosis. The cell remains active, and the chromosomes stay in their replicated state while the cellular machinery needed for division is readied. In typical animal cells, centrosome duplication is completed by this period. Mira places that statement beside an animal-cell model rather than stamping it onto every eukaryotic cell, preserving the biological context of the canonical relationship.

The center bay grows busier, yet the chromosome case itself does not acquire another sister copy. Mira makes you compare the left entry state with the right exit state. Replicated chromosomes entered. Replicated chromosomes leave. G2 is preparation after DNA synthesis, not a second S phase. If the archive copied the DNA again here, the cell would enter mitosis with the wrong DNA content. The visual rule is therefore simple: S copied; G2 prepares. Mira seals the S-phase hatch with a numbered tag and asks you to compare the replicated chromosome case before and after several rounds of G2 protein synthesis. The cell changes its preparation state, but the sister-chromatid structure in the case does not gain another copy. This makes G2 a preparation interval with a stable post-S chromosome state.

A diagnostic light appears at the M gate, indicating that later cell-cycle control can determine whether a cell should proceed. Mira does not turn this room into the G2 checkpoint. That checkpoint belongs to the regulation journey that follows mitosis. The **G2 phase** itself is the period of continued synthesis and preparation. Phase and checkpoint must remain separate in the learner's mental map just as they did at G1.

When the right-hand M gate finally glows, the case seems ready to move forward. But the central cell-cycle clock projects a side corridor from the G1 region, flashing G0. The archive has filed G0 as 'dead end: permanent stop.' Mira blocks the M gate with her tablet. 'Before we divide these chromosomes, there is one side branch we have to correct,' she says. She keeps the replicated case safely parked beside the M gate and leads you into the **G0 Side Chamber** to inspect what a nondividing state actually means.

**Exit**  G2 ends with replicated chromosomes intact and the cell prepared for mitosis without a second round of DNA synthesis. Before the tracked set enters M phase, the archive must correct its separate G0 branch so nondividing states are not mistaken for universal permanent endpoints.

## 8. G0 Side Chamber

### The Side Chamber That Was Not Always a Dead End

*G0 is a nondividing state whose reversibility depends on cell type and context.*

**Physical layout**  On your left is Cell-cycle exit. Directly ahead is G0 nondividing state. On your right is Reentry or long-term nondividing outcome. These anchors remain fixed while the chromosome/cell-cycle mechanism changes.

A narrow side door opens away from the main cycle into the **G0 Side Chamber**. The geometry is immediately different from the forward-moving G1–S–G2 route. On your **left**, an EXIT arrow branches away from the active cell-cycle path. Directly **ahead**, a quiet but fully functioning cell sits in the central G0 chamber, carrying out specialized cellular work while no division clock advances. On your **right**, the corridor splits again: one gate curves back toward the active cycle, while another continues into a long-term differentiated state. Mira points to the old archive sign bolted over both routes: PERMANENTLY STOPPED. 'That label is too absolute,' she says.

She branches a model cell out of a G1-associated decision and into the center chamber. The cell stops progressing through the division cycle, yet its ordinary cellular functions continue. Mira names this the **G0 stage**. The paired idea of **G0 stage and reentry** is visible in the room itself: a cell can occupy this nondividing state, and some cell types can later reenter the cell cycle after appropriate cues. A cell in G0 is in a nondividing state outside the active division cycle. The room is quiet only with respect to cell-cycle progression; it is not a model of a biologically inactive or dead cell. The same distinction applies to **nondividing cells** more generally: they may exit the cycle or remain held in a nondividing state while continuing other cellular functions.

Mira now tests the two right-hand routes. One example cell receives appropriate cues, leaves the G0 chamber, and passes through the reentry gate toward the active cycle. Some cell types can therefore **reenter** the cell cycle. A second example remains in a differentiated, nondividing state for a long period. The two outcomes coexist in the same room so the learner cannot memorize 'G0 means forever' or the opposite claim that every G0 cell readily reenters. Reversibility depends on cell type and biological context.

She removes the PERMANENTLY STOPPED sign and replaces it with NONDIVIDING—VARIABLE REVERSIBILITY. The left exit, center G0 state, and right reentry/long-term routes remain fixed. Then Mira asks you to reconstruct the whole preparation archive backward. G0 sits outside the active G1–S–G2–M loop. G1 is growth and normal activity before DNA synthesis. S replicates DNA. G2 prepares for mitosis after replication. Through all of those changes, chromosome identity remains traceable and DNA replication does not automatically mean chromosome number doubled.

Mira returns to the main corridor and lifts the transparent chromosome case from beside the M gate. Inside, the replicated chromosomes still consist of sister chromatids held together. The case has survived every filing correction: DNA and histones formed chromatin; chromosome anatomy separated centromere from kinetochore; homologs were distinguished from sister copies; ploidy was separated from cell type; the cell-cycle clock separated interphase from M phase; and S phase doubled DNA without inventing new chromosome identities. Ahead, steel rails descend toward a hall where spindle fibers are already assembling.

The archive doors close behind you, but the chromosome case remains in Mira's hands. 'Preparation is finished,' she says. 'Now we watch these same sister chromatids move.' The next sign illuminates: **Mitosis Transit Hall**.

**Exit**  The G0 branch is corrected as a nondividing state with variable reversibility. The journey ends with the same replicated chromosome set intact and ready for mitosis, carrying forward stable distinctions among genome, chromatin, chromosome, sister chromatid, homolog, ploidy, and G1–S–G2–G0 states.

## Journey payoff

At the archive exit, the learner can reconstruct one continuous chromosome identity through packaging and the cell cycle. Histones help package DNA into nucleosomes and chromatin; chromosome anatomy separates sister chromatids, centromere, and kinetochore; homologs are not sister copies; diploid/haploid describe chromosome-set number; interphase contains active G1, S, and G2; S phase doubles DNA content and chromatid number without doubling centromere-counted chromosome number before sister separation; G2 prepares for mitosis; and G0 is a nondividing state with variable reversibility. The same replicated chromosome case then enters Mitosis Transit Hall.
