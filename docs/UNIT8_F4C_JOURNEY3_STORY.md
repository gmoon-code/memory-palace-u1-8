# Unit 8 Journey 3 Narrative Source

## The Watershed with the Missing Arrows

### 1. Watershed Reservoir Map Hub · The Boxes That Refused to Move

You enter the Watershed Reservoir Map Hub at the crest of a broad indoor watershed model. On your left, a wall holds fixed boxes labeled for abiotic and biotic pools, including atmosphere, water, soil, rock, plants, animals, and decomposers. Ahead at the center, an empty metal grid waits for transfer arrows to be slotted between those boxes. On your right, a clear destination box holds one glass tracer bead that is chemically unchanged from the bead that began on the left. Dr. Mira Sen places the same transparent watershed reservoir board you carried from the energy hall on the rail in front of you. Its pool boxes are fixed in place, and every pathway between them is blank.

A warning light above the map reads ARROWS MISSING. Mira does not reach for the labels. She taps one of the left boxes and asks you to treat it as a place where matter can remain for some period. A carbon atom can remain in atmospheric carbon dioxide, water can remain in an ocean, nitrogen can remain in atmospheric N₂, and phosphate can remain in rock or sediment. The amount of time can vary widely. What matters first is that the location is a pool, not a process. Mira slides the tracer into the atmosphere box and leaves it there. Nothing is moving, so no arrow appears. Only after the bead has clearly stayed in one place does she name the role. A component of a cycle in which matter can remain for a period before transfer to another pool is a **reservoir**.

Now the center grid activates. Mira places a card labeled PHOTOSYNTHESIS into one arrow slot between atmosphere and plant. The arrow lights, and the tracer moves from the atmosphere box to the plant box on the right side of that route. The process stays printed on the arrow. It never becomes a reservoir. She removes the process card, and the tracer stops. Next she inserts FEEDING between plant and animal, then DECOMPOSITION between an organism and soil. Each time, the boxes remain fixed while the tracer changes location only through a named transfer. The board makes the distinction impossible to miss. Reservoirs are where matter can stay. Transfer processes are what move matter among reservoirs.

Mira turns the board so you can see both kinds of pools together. Some reservoirs are abiotic, such as atmosphere, water, soil, rock, and sediment. Others are biotic, such as living plants, animals, and microorganisms. Matter can move between abiotic and biotic reservoirs through specific processes. The route does not have to be one perfect circle, and different ecosystems can have different reservoir sizes and different fluxes. The useful structure is the same. Identify the matter, identify where it is stored, identify the process that transfers it, and follow the same material into the next reservoir without pretending that the process itself is a pool.

The four tracer cases on the far wall unlock one at a time. One holds water, one carbon, one nitrogen, and one phosphorus. Mira leaves their names covered. She wants the shared architecture to become stable before the chemistry changes. A cycle that tracks the movement of a chemical element or compound among biotic and abiotic reservoirs through transfer processes is a **biogeochemical cycle**. The word is long, but the map underneath it is simple enough to redraw. Fixed pools on the left, a transfer arrow in the center, the same matter arriving in a destination reservoir on the right.

A floor channel opens toward the next chamber. The blue water tracer rolls into a clear trough while the other three remain locked in their cases. Mira does not carry the arrow labels with you. Only the reservoir board comes forward. The challenge in the next room is to rebuild the pathways from the behavior of water itself. If you remember only one picture from this hub, keep the boxes still and move the tracer only when a real process earns an arrow.

### 2. Water Cycle Watershed · The Storm Above the Glass Valley

You step into the Water Cycle Watershed and stand at the edge of a waist-high glass valley. On your left, a deep ocean basin, a shallow lake, a stream, and a row of living plants hold the starting water reservoirs. Ahead in the center, warm lamps, a transparent atmosphere chamber, cooling plates, and cloud nozzles form an evaporation, transpiration, condensation, and precipitation loop. On your right, sloping soil, porous gravel, a groundwater tank, and a returning stream channel show runoff, infiltration, groundwater, and stream pathways. The same watershed reservoir board locks beside the model, with its boxes fixed and its arrow slots empty.

Mira releases the first blue tracer into the ocean basin. The warm lamp comes on, and a fine mist rises from the water surface into the clear atmosphere chamber. The tracer marker on the board shifts from surface water to atmosphere only after the upward arrow is labeled **evaporation**. Beside the basin, the plant roots take up water and a second mist leaves the stomata of the leaves. That path receives a different label, **transpiration**. Both routes move water into the atmosphere, but one begins at exposed water surfaces and the other passes through organisms. The water molecule has not become a new kind of matter merely because its physical state or location changed.

Cold plates above the valley cool the rising vapor. Droplets appear, join, and cling to the underside of the chamber. Mira waits until you can see water collecting before the next arrow receives the word **condensation**. Then the nozzles release a brief artificial storm. Water falls back toward the land and surface reservoirs as **precipitation**. The central loop now has four clear process names, each attached to motion between reservoirs. The board does not suggest that every droplet must follow one identical circle. It shows multiple valid routes through a shared system.

That becomes obvious on the right. Some rain strikes the steep slope and runs over the surface into the stream as **runoff**. Some enters spaces in the soil as **infiltration**. A portion continues downward into the groundwater tank, then moves slowly toward the stream from below. Mira shines a narrow light through the gravel so you can watch the blue tracer appear beneath the land long after the surface flow has passed. Groundwater and streams are reservoirs and pathways within the larger movement of water, not missing details to be squeezed into the central four-arrow loop.

Only after the storm has passed does Mira uncover the room name. The movement of water among oceans, surface waters, atmosphere, and organisms through processes including evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and transpiration is the **water cycle**. Runoff, infiltration, groundwater, and streams add important return pathways. You glance back at the reservoir board and see the same rule from the first room working again. The boxes hold matter. The process names sit on the arrows. The blue tracer is still water wherever it goes.

At the far end of the valley, the water drains through a grate. Behind it, a terrace glows under greenhouse lamps. The next tracer case opens and reveals black carbon markers. Mira leaves the blue water bead in its final reservoir and slides the carbon set onto the same board. She points out that the shared reservoir-transfer grammar will remain, while the dominant reservoirs and transformations will change. Water moved readily through the atmosphere as vapor and precipitation. Carbon will require you to watch organisms, stored carbon, and chemical exchange much more closely.

### 3. Carbon Cycle Reservoir Terrace · The Tree Between Air and Fire

You climb onto the Carbon Cycle Reservoir Terrace and stop beside a living tree rooted in a deep soil column. On your left, a transparent chamber labeled ATMOSPHERIC CO₂ hangs above the canopy, while sealed drawers beneath the floor represent longer-lived stored-carbon reservoirs. Ahead in the center, illuminated arrows connect the atmosphere, green leaves, living tissues, decomposers, and soil through photosynthesis, cellular respiration, and decomposition. On your right, a combustion chamber faces a returned-CO₂ panel that feeds back toward the atmosphere. The watershed reservoir board sits beside the tree with carbon tracer markers ready to move through the same fixed pool-and-process framework.

Mira begins with one carbon marker inside atmospheric CO₂. The greenhouse lamps brighten, and the leaf display starts tracking carbon atoms incorporated into organic molecules. Only then does the arrow from atmosphere into plant tissue light with **photosynthesis**. Carbon dioxide has moved into organic matter. Mira keeps the energy column on her transparent ecology tablet separate from the matter tracer. Light energy supports the chemical reactions, yet the carbon being tracked is matter. The scene cannot be reduced to energy cycling. The carbon atom remains traceable even as chemical bonds and molecular forms change.

The tree and a small animal chamber begin cellular respiration. Carbon markers leave organic molecules and return to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide. The arrow is labeled **cellular respiration**. In the soil column, dead leaves fall into a decomposer chamber. Microorganisms break down organic matter, and carbon dioxide returns through **decomposition**. Mira has you compare those two arrows with photosynthesis. Photosynthesis removes atmospheric carbon dioxide into organic matter. Cellular respiration and decomposition return carbon dioxide from organic matter. The same carbon can enter and leave living reservoirs through different processes.

Then the room lights dim except for the sealed drawers beneath the floor. They represent carbon that can remain stored for much longer periods in geological or other reservoirs. Mira transfers several markers into one drawer and closes it. Nothing happens until she activates the combustion chamber on the right. The stored carbon markers move rapidly to the returned-CO₂ panel and then toward the atmosphere. **Combustion** can release stored carbon much faster than many natural storage processes accumulated it. The pathway differs in timescale and mechanism from the biological exchange you watched around the tree.

The full map is now crowded with carbon pathways but still obeys the original architecture. Atmospheric carbon dioxide, organic matter, soil carbon, and stored carbon are reservoirs. Photosynthesis, cellular respiration, decomposition, and combustion are transfers. This movement of carbon through the biosphere and atmosphere is the **carbon cycle**. Mira runs one finger along the arrows and asks you to explain the cycle without describing it as a single circular conveyor. You can begin anywhere, name the reservoir, name the transfer process, and follow the carbon into another reservoir.

A steel door opens behind the returned-CO₂ panel. Through it you can see a long gallery with nitrogen symbols printed above five separate gates. Mira removes the carbon tracer from the board and replaces it with a marker labeled N. She warns you that the next room punishes vague arrows. Water could often be followed as the same H₂O molecule through changes in location and state. Carbon could be tracked through several familiar biological exchanges. Nitrogen will require attention to chemical form. The reservoir board stays with you, but the center arrows are about to become a chemically directional pathway.

### 4. Nitrogen Transformation Gallery · Five Gates for One Element

You enter the Nitrogen Transformation Gallery and stand before a long illuminated pathway. On your left, a tall cylinder marked ATMOSPHERIC N₂ rises beside a bin of organic nitrogen in dead matter and waste. Ahead in the center, three connected stations display ammonium, nitrite, and nitrate as distinct chemical forms rather than interchangeable labels. On your right, one doorway leads nitrogen into living organic molecules through assimilation, while another returns gaseous nitrogen forms to the atmosphere. The watershed reservoir board remains fixed beside the track, but every transfer arrow is dark until its chemical direction is earned.

Mira starts at the atmospheric N₂ cylinder. The molecule is abundant in the atmosphere, yet most organisms cannot simply pull N₂ directly into proteins or nucleic acids. A microbial chamber activates and converts atmospheric N₂ into reduced nitrogen forms that can enter biological systems. Only after that conversion occurs does the first gate illuminate with the name **nitrogen fixation**. Mira moves the resulting inorganic nitrogen marker toward the right-side plant chamber. Roots take up inorganic nitrogen and incorporate it into organic molecules. That biological incorporation receives its own name, **nitrogen assimilation**. Fixation changes atmospheric N₂ into biologically accessible reduced forms. Assimilation incorporates inorganic nitrogen into organic molecules in organisms. The two gates are connected, but they are not the same process.

Next Mira points to the organic nitrogen bin on the left. Dead tissue and waste enter a decomposer chamber. Microorganisms convert organic nitrogen into ammonium. The moment the ammonium marker appears, a second pathway label lights with **ammonification**. The name stays attached to the conversion from organic nitrogen to ammonium. Mira physically places the organic-N card before the gate and the ammonium card after it so the direction cannot be reversed in memory. This is matter being chemically transformed and transferred, not a general label for all decomposition.

The ammonium marker then enters the center track. Microorganisms oxidize ammonium to nitrite and then nitrate. Two redox steps illuminate in sequence, but the whole microbial oxidation pathway is labeled **nitrification**. Mira keeps ammonium, nitrite, and nitrate in separate slots. She does not let the three forms collapse into one vague box called soil nitrogen. The chemical direction matters. Nitrification begins with ammonium and proceeds through nitrite to nitrate. The nitrate can then be available for assimilation by producers, or it can enter a different microbial pathway under suitable conditions.

At the final gate, nitrate enters a chamber where microbes reduce it to gaseous nitrogen forms. A pipe rises from the chamber to the atmospheric N₂ cylinder. Only when the gas reaches the left-side atmospheric reservoir does the gate display **denitrification**. The word tells you what happened to nitrate and where the nitrogen can go. Nitrate was reduced to gaseous nitrogen forms, returning nitrogen toward the atmosphere. Mira now has you point to all five gates without relying on their names. Atmospheric N₂ to reduced nitrogen is fixation. Inorganic nitrogen into organic molecules is assimilation. Organic nitrogen to ammonium is ammonification. Ammonium to nitrite and nitrate is nitrification. Nitrate to gaseous nitrogen forms is denitrification.

The full network brightens. It is not one compulsory circular conveyor, and organisms do not choose a route because they need nitrogen to move in a particular direction. Microorganisms mediate several key transformations, and different nitrogen forms occupy different reservoirs and pathways. The movement of nitrogen through fixation, assimilation, ammonification, nitrification, and denitrification is the **nitrogen cycle**. Mira leaves the five process names above their gates and lowers the lights everywhere else. If you can reconstruct the direction of each chemical transformation, the names become labels on a mechanism you already understand.

A final door opens at floor level rather than overhead. Beyond it lies a rocky basin with no large atmospheric chamber at all. Mira smiles and taps the empty sky above the model. The absence is deliberate. The phosphorus tracer waits in a block of rock on the left. The reservoir board rolls forward one last time, ready to show a cycle whose dominant architecture is sedimentary rather than atmospheric.

### 5. Phosphorus Sedimentary Basin · The Cycle with No Great Sky Reservoir

You descend into the Phosphorus Sedimentary Basin and stand beside a rock-soil-water-food-web cross-section. On your left, phosphate-bearing rock rises above layered sediment reservoirs at the bottom of the basin. Ahead in the center, arrows for weathering, producer uptake, feeding, decomposition, and excretion cross from rock into soil and water, then through plants and animals. On your right, soil, shallow water, and accumulating sediment receive returning phosphate. Above all three zones, the ceiling is deliberately empty. There is no giant atmospheric tank waiting to dominate this cycle.

Mira fixes a phosphate tracer inside the rock on the left. A simulated rain system starts, and the exposed rock surface slowly breaks down. Phosphate moves into the soil and water only when the arrow labeled **weathering** illuminates. The process is slow compared with the rapid movement you saw in some earlier rooms, yet it is a crucial route by which phosphate becomes available from rock. The phosphorus atom itself is conserved as it changes location and chemical context. The reservoir board still distinguishes the stationary pools from the processes that connect them.

A plant root reaches into the soil reservoir and takes up phosphate. Mira moves the tracer into the producer and marks the transfer as assimilation into biological material. An herbivore feeds on the plant, carrying that phosphorus into a consumer. Another consumer feeds farther along the food web. The movement through organisms happens through uptake and feeding, not through a major atmospheric phase. Mira briefly points to the empty ceiling again. Nitrogen had a huge atmospheric N₂ reservoir and processes that connected the atmosphere to biologically available forms. Phosphorus does not have an equivalently large atmospheric reservoir in its major ecological cycle.

Dead material and waste now enter the decomposer zone. Phosphate returns to soil or water through decomposition and excretion. Some phosphate is transported into aquatic systems and ultimately settles into sediments. Over long periods, sedimentary processes can lock phosphorus into rock again. Mira asks you to compare this route with the water, carbon, and nitrogen rooms without borrowing their distinctive mechanisms. There is no evaporation-condensation loop for phosphorus. There is no photosynthesis-respiration exchange that defines the carbon cycle. There is no sequence of nitrogen fixation, nitrification, and denitrification. Each cycle uses the same reservoir-transfer logic while preserving its own dominant reservoirs and transformations.

Only after the rocky route is complete does the basin wall reveal its name. Weathering releases phosphate, producers assimilate it, consumers acquire it through feeding, and decomposition and excretion return it to environmental reservoirs. This movement is the **phosphorus cycle**. Its **sedimentary character** is visible in the importance of rock and sediment reservoirs and in the absence of a large atmospheric reservoir. Mira does not call the cycle perfectly closed or identical in every ecosystem. Reservoir sizes, transfer rates, and specific pathways can vary. What remains stable is the scientific distinction between where phosphate can remain and the processes that move it.

The basin doors open back onto a balcony overlooking all five locations. The original map hub lies closest, followed by the glass water valley, the carbon terrace, the nitrogen gallery, and the rocky phosphorus basin. Mira places the four tracer sets side by side on the transparent watershed reservoir board. Water emphasizes evaporation, condensation, precipitation, transpiration, runoff, infiltration, groundwater, and streams. Carbon emphasizes photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, and combustion. Nitrogen requires chemical direction through fixation, assimilation, ammonification, nitrification, and denitrification. Phosphorus emphasizes weathering, uptake, feeding, decomposition, excretion, and sedimentation without a large atmospheric reservoir. The missing arrows are no longer missing. You rebuilt them from reservoirs and processes, which means you can rebuild the cycles even when the diagram changes.
