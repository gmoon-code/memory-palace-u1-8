# Unit 8 Journey 7 Narrative Source

## The Observatory Where Equal Counts Told Different Stories

### 1. Community Composition Counter · The Two Trays That Looked Equally Diverse

You enter the Community Composition Counter through a glass door overlooking two living meadow enclosures. On your left, a brass tray holds the species richness count for Sample A. Ahead at the center, two matched species-count trays display the relative abundance distribution for Samples A and B with one compartment for each species. On your right, a tall interpretation panel waits to judge the combined species-diversity pattern. Dr. Mira Sen clips one fixed community sample card to the rail between the trays. The card has spaces for each species count, total N, a diversity readout, and an intervention panel that will stay with you through the observatory.

At first glance the two samples seem identical. Each tray contains four labeled species compartments, and each compartment contains at least one individual marker. Mira asks you to count only the number of species represented. Sample A contains four species. Sample B also contains four species. She writes four on the left panel for each tray. The number of species represented in a community or sample is **Species richness**. Both samples therefore have the same species richness. The first measurement has answered one question, yet it has not told you how the individuals are distributed among those four species.

Mira slides the trays under the center lamp. Sample A contains twenty-five individuals of each of the four species. Sample B contains ninety-one individuals of one species and three individuals of each of the other three species. Both trays still contain one hundred individuals in total. Mira asks you to compare the fraction of the community belonging to each species. In Sample A, each species represents one quarter of the individuals. In Sample B, one species dominates almost the whole tray while the other three are rare. The proportion of individuals in a community represented by each species is **Relative abundance**. The center display now shows four nearly equal bars for Sample A and one towering bar beside three short bars for Sample B.

The right panel finally wakes. It refuses to copy the number four from the richness display. Instead it pulls information from both the left and the center. Mira explains that **Species diversity** incorporates both species richness and the relative abundance distribution of species. The two samples have equal richness, yet their abundance distributions are sharply different. Sample A spreads individuals evenly across the four species. Sample B is dominated by one species. The right panel therefore treats the two communities as different in species diversity even though a simple species count made them look the same.

To make the distinction harder to lose, Mira covers the species names and leaves only the counts. She asks what each station can legitimately tell you. The left station can tell you how many species are represented. The center station can tell you what proportion of individuals belongs to each species. The right station combines those two kinds of information into species-diversity reasoning. None of the three stations is allowed to impersonate another. A community with more species can differ from one with fewer species, and two communities with the same richness can still differ when their relative abundance distributions differ.

Mira attaches Sample A to the community sample card and leaves Sample B visible beside it as a comparison. The card now carries four species counts and total N, while the diversity readout remains blank. A narrow passage opens toward a calculation table where the same counts will be fed into an equation. Mira warns you that the next room will use the symbols n and N, and the observatory will lock if you treat them as the same quantity. The trays roll forward without changing a single count.

### 2. Simpson Index Calculation Table · The Lowercase Letter That Could Not Replace the Capital

You enter the Simpson Index Calculation Table with Sample A still clipped to the same community card. On your left, four species-specific n counts sit in separate illuminated cells. Ahead at the center, the total N and the equation 1 − Σ(n/N)² are etched into a transparent calculation surface. On your right, an interpretation scale runs from lower diversity toward greater diversity for this AP form of the index. Mira places Sample B beside the table as a comparison and locks both total community sizes at one hundred so the only visible difference remains the abundance distribution.

Mira taps the first species count in Sample A. Twenty-five individuals belong to that species. She places 25 into the lowercase n position. Then she points to the total of all organisms in the sample and places 100 into capital N. She repeats the process for each species. The phrase **n in Simpson diversity index** stays printed beside the left cells to remind you that n is the number of individuals of a particular species. The phrase **N in Simpson diversity index** stays printed beside the total box to remind you that N is the total number of organisms of all species in the community sample. Lowercase n changes from species to species. Capital N stays tied to the whole sample.

The center surface begins the calculation pipeline. For each species, the table forms n divided by N, squares that proportion, and adds the squared proportions across all species. Mira does not let the arithmetic become the main event. She keeps pointing to what each number means. A large squared proportion signals dominance by a species. Several similarly sized proportions spread that contribution more evenly. After the table sums the squared proportions, it subtracts the sum from 1. The full relationship remains visible as **Simpson diversity index** with the AP Biology equation 1 − Σ(n/N)².

Sample A sends four equal proportions through the pipeline. Sample B sends one very large proportion and three very small ones. The large dominant proportion in Sample B contributes a large squared term, which drives the summed dominance measure upward. Because the AP form subtracts that sum from 1, stronger dominance pushes the final index downward. The right scale therefore places Sample A higher than Sample B. For the AP form 1 − Σ(n/N)², larger values indicate greater diversity because dominance by a few species lowers the index.

Mira then swaps the labels on the n and N boxes without changing the numbers. The table flashes red. A species-specific count cannot suddenly become the total community count, and the total community count cannot be reused as though it belonged to one species. She restores the labels and asks you to explain the equation from left to right. You point to a particular species count for n, the total sample size for N, the squared proportion for each species, the sum across species, and the subtraction from 1. The formula now reads like a measurement process rather than a string of symbols.

The community card records the resulting diversity readout beside the original species counts. Mira leaves the actual arithmetic practice for a later application task and keeps the interpretation attached to the visible sample. A stairway rises toward the observatory dome. Through the glass above, three larger panels are waiting. One shows variation within populations, one shows differences among species, and one shows different ecosystems across a landscape. The next room will widen the question from species diversity inside one community sample to biodiversity across several biological levels.

### 3. Biodiversity Resilience Observatory · The Dome That Refused to Promise Recovery

You enter the Biodiversity Resilience Observatory beneath a wide circular dome. On your left, three separate panels display genetic diversity, species diversity, and ecosystem diversity. Ahead at the center, a disturbance simulator is prepared to impose the same type of environmental challenge on several model systems. On your right, a resilience and function panel records how much ecological function persists and how recovery proceeds after the challenge. Mira locks the community sample card into the left console so the species-diversity measurement from the previous room remains visible as only one part of the larger picture.

The left wall expands the scale. One panel shows variation among individuals within a population. A second shows the species composition and abundance pattern of a community. A third shows a landscape containing different ecosystem types. Mira waits until you can point to all three levels before naming the umbrella concept. **Biodiversity** includes variation at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels. Species diversity is one component of biodiversity. The species counts and Simpson readout on your card therefore describe a meaningful part of biodiversity, yet they do not capture genetic variation within populations or the variety of ecosystems across a region.

The center simulator now applies the same kind of disturbance to paired model systems. In one pair, the community has fewer component species and fewer functional alternatives. In the other, more species contribute overlapping or complementary ecological roles. The disturbance removes one resource pathway. The first model loses a larger share of its measured function. The second retains more function because some remaining species can still perform parts of the affected process. Mira records the pattern carefully. Ecosystems with fewer component parts and little diversity are often less resilient to change. In many contexts, greater biological diversity can increase resilience and some ecosystem functions.

Then Mira deliberately changes the disturbance. A new challenge targets a feature shared by nearly every species in the more diverse model. This time the system with greater diversity does not receive automatic protection. The right panel flashes a warning across the recovery graph. The diversity-resilience relationship is a tendency that depends on ecological context, the kinds of organisms present, their interactions, the function being measured, and the disturbance itself. Greater biological diversity can increase resilience and some ecosystem functions in many contexts, but the relationship is not an unconditional guarantee for every ecosystem or disturbance.

Mira adds three maintenance markers to the left display. Producers supply the biological foundation for energy entry into many ecosystems. Essential abiotic and biotic factors shape whether populations can persist. Certain species can exert unusually strong effects on community structure. Together these examples show that the components that maintain diversity are not interchangeable decorations. Keystone species, producers, and essential abiotic and biotic factors can help maintain ecosystem diversity, yet the size and direction of their effects depend on the system being examined.

The dome darkens except for the fixed community sample card. Its species counts, total N, and diversity readout are still there. Mira opens the intervention panel that has remained unused until now. Two removal controls appear. One targets a rare species with a surprisingly large web of interaction lines. The other targets an abundant habitat-forming species that physically creates much of the living structure around it. The final room will ask you to judge ecological influence relative to abundance and to separate two kinds of strong community effect that are easy to collapse into one label.

### 4. Community Influence Removal Test · The Rare Species That Held the Room Together

You enter the Community Influence Removal Test and step onto a platform surrounding two matched experimental tanks. On your left, a species abundance and habitat-forming panel shows how common each species is and whether any species creates much of the physical habitat. Ahead at the center, the intervention controls can remove or greatly reduce one species at a time. On your right, a community-structure response panel tracks prey abundance, competitor abundance, habitat complexity, species persistence, and interaction-network change. Mira places the same community sample card beneath the controls so the diversity measurements from the earlier rooms remain attached to the community being tested.

The first tank contains a low-abundance predator represented by only a small fraction of the organisms counted on the left. Mira asks you to predict its importance from abundance alone. It looks minor. She reduces the predator sharply. A prey population rises, several attached competitors decline, and the right panel shows a cascade of changes across the interaction network. The response is much larger than the predator's original abundance would have suggested. Mira names the pattern **Keystone species**. A keystone species has a disproportionately large effect on community structure relative to its abundance.

She restores the predator and repeats the removal slowly so you can watch cause and consequence. The predator itself was never numerous, yet its interactions constrained other populations. When that constraint weakens, indirect effects spread through the community. Mira labels the visible result **Keystone species effect**. A keystone species can have an effect on community structure disproportionate to its abundance, and its removal can cause major ecosystem change. The term is tied to evidence from this interaction network. It is not awarded because the species is famous, charismatic, or widely discussed.

The second tank looks different even before any intervention. One organism is extremely abundant and creates much of the three-dimensional habitat used by other species. When Mira reduces it, shelter and attachment surfaces disappear and many populations decline. The effect is also large, yet its route is different. A foundation species can strongly shape a community because of high abundance or habitat-forming effects. That differs from the classic low-abundance keystone concept. The left panel keeps abundance and habitat formation visible so a large community effect cannot automatically be labeled keystone.

Mira then projects the same taxon into a different community where its interaction network is weaker. Its removal produces only modest change. She does the reverse with another species that becomes highly influential in a different food web. Whether a taxon functions as a keystone species depends on the ecological community and interaction network. A label that fits one ecosystem cannot be stamped permanently onto a taxon everywhere. Coral, honey bees, predators, or any other familiar example must be evaluated in the ecological context being studied.

The right panel now links back to every room in the observatory. Richness tells how many species are represented. Relative abundance tells how the individuals are distributed among those species. Species diversity combines those two dimensions. Simpson diversity index turns the abundance pattern into the AP form 1 − Σ(n/N)². Biodiversity widens the view to genetic, species, and ecosystem variation. Resilience describes how a system responds to change and cannot be guaranteed from diversity alone. Keystone and foundation effects reveal how particular community components can matter for very different reasons.

Mira closes the intervention panel and hands you the completed community sample card. Nothing on it stands alone. Counts acquire meaning through proportions. Proportions feed a diversity measure. Diversity sits inside a broader biodiversity framework. Disturbance tests expose conditional resilience. Species-removal tests reveal influence that may be disproportionate to abundance or may arise from habitat-forming dominance. The observatory exit opens only after you can reconstruct that sequence from the card without treating a single number or familiar species name as the whole ecological story.
